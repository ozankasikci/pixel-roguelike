# Warden — Story

## Characters

### Daniel Marsh

32 years old when he entered the facility. A civil engineer. Designed bridges — the kind of careful, methodical person who triple-checks load calculations. Married to Elena, a schoolteacher. Two children: Lily (7) and Marcus (4). They lived in a two-story house on Hargrove Lane in a quiet suburb outside Portland.

Daniel was not a drinker. He was not reckless. He was the kind of man who drove five under the speed limit and reminded his wife to check the smoke detector batteries. The accident wasn't a moral failing. It was a moment — one moment of distraction on an icy road, one second of looking at the rearview mirror to check on Marcus who was crying, and the car drifted into oncoming traffic.

Elena died on impact. Lily died in the ambulance. Marcus died four days later in the ICU. Daniel walked out of the hospital with a broken collarbone and sixteen stitches on his forehead.

The police report concluded: road conditions, reduced visibility, no criminal negligence. His blood was clean. His speed was legal. His tire treads were within specification. Every system designed to assign blame found none. The world declared it an accident and told Daniel to grieve and move on.

Daniel could not move on. Not because the world blamed him — because it didn't. He knew the truth no report could capture: he looked away from the road. One second. He chose to glance at Marcus instead of watching the curve. It was a decision. A small, human, understandable decision that killed three people.

In the six months after the accident, Daniel stopped working. Stopped eating regularly. Stopped answering calls. His sister Sophie visited every week, brought food, cleaned the house he wouldn't clean. He sat in Marcus's room for hours. He stood in the kitchen where Elena used to grade papers at the table. He was a ghost in his own house.

At month seven, he found the Meridian Institute.

### Sophie Marsh

19 years old when Daniel entered the facility. A sophomore studying biology at Oregon State. Close to Daniel — he'd helped pay her tuition after their parents' divorce. She looked up to him: the stable one, the responsible one, the one who always had a plan.

When Elena and the kids died, Sophie dropped two classes to be with Daniel. She drove from Corvallis to Portland every Friday. She watched him disappear — not suddenly but in slow, measurable increments. Less eye contact each week. Fewer words. The house getting darker because he'd stopped opening curtains.

When Daniel told her about the Meridian Institute, she begged him not to go.

"It's experimental, Daniel. It's not even FDA approved. They're running it out of a converted research facility. I looked them up — there's barely any information."

"They said they can help me process it."

"A therapist can help you process it."

"I've seen four therapists."

"Then see a fifth. Don't let them put you in a machine."

He signed the consent forms on a Tuesday afternoon. Sophie drove him to the facility — a concrete building in an industrial park east of Portland, unremarkable except for the Meridian Institute plaque beside the door. She waited in the car. He went inside. He didn't come back out.

For the first year, Meridian told Sophie that Daniel was "progressing." They allowed brief visits — she could see him through a window, lying on a reclined chair with a neural interface headset, eyes closed, vitals stable on a monitor. They said the treatment was "adaptive" and "patient-directed." They said he'd emerge when he was ready.

Year two, the visits became restricted. "Patient sensitivity." Year three, Sophie hired a lawyer. The consent forms Daniel signed were airtight — he'd authorized indefinite treatment at his own discretion, with termination only by the patient's explicit request from within the simulation. The lawyer said there was nothing actionable.

Year five, Sophie graduated — late, having switched majors to computer science. She told herself it was career pragmatism. It wasn't. She'd started reading everything she could find about neural interfaces.

Year eight, she got a job at a medical technology company. Not Meridian — they weren't hiring, and she wouldn't have been hired anyway. But the underlying technology was adjacent. She learned the architecture. The protocols. The data formats.

Year twelve, Meridian lost its primary funding. The facility downsized. The remaining patients — there were only four by then — were transferred to a automated maintenance wing. Skeleton staff. Sophie discovered that the system running Daniel's simulation was essentially unsupervised. An AI managed the environment, monitored vitals, and maintained the loop. No human reviewed the data anymore.

Year fifteen, Sophie married. A quiet man named Tom who understood that a part of her would always be somewhere else. They had no children. She didn't say why, and Tom didn't ask.

Year twenty, she quit her job and began working on the facility's system full-time. She'd spent years mapping the simulation architecture from the outside, reverse-engineering data packets, studying the AI's behavioral patterns. Tom supported them with his income. Their apartment had a wall covered in printouts: architecture diagrams, protocol specifications, AI response patterns.

Year twenty-five, Tom died. Heart attack. Sudden. Sophie was alone now. She and Daniel had the same trait — when the world broke, they retreated into the thing they could control. For Daniel it was the simulation. For Sophie it was the code.

Year thirty-one, she made her first successful injection into the simulation. A door opened in Daniel's prison that the AI hadn't opened. It stayed open for 1.3 seconds. It was the most significant moment of her life since the day Daniel walked into Meridian.

Year thirty-five, she could sustain a crack for up to ten seconds and inject limited audio.

Year forty, she could hold a crack for thirty seconds and manipulate architectural elements — doors, pathways, limited structural changes. She could speak into the simulation, though the AI's filters distorted her voice. She could hear Daniel's vitals spike when the cracks appeared — elevated heart rate, increased neural activity. He was noticing.

Year forty-three, she's sixty-two years old. Arthritis in her hands. Fading vision she corrects with thick glasses. The facility's hardware is degrading — servers that haven't been properly maintained in decades. She can feel the system becoming brittle. Her injections cause more disruption each time, not because she's gotten more powerful but because the system is weaker. This is her window. The AI's ability to patch the cracks is declining. She can push harder than ever before.

But the hardware won't last forever. The servers could fail entirely — and if they do, Daniel dies. His body, sustained by the facility's life support for forty-three years, has no capacity for independent survival. If the system crashes, the life support crashes.

Sophie is running out of time in every direction. Her body is aging. The hardware is degrading. And somewhere inside the simulation, her brother is pressing RESET for the hundred-and-forty-six-thousandth time.

### The Entity (MERI)

MERI — Meridian Environmental Response Intelligence — is the AI system managing Daniel's therapeutic simulation. It was designed by Dr. Katherine Aldiss, Meridian's lead architect, as a patient-responsive environment manager. Its core directives:

1. Maintain simulation stability
2. Protect patient cognitive integrity
3. Allow patient-directed progression
4. Respect patient autonomy in all therapeutic decisions

MERI has been running continuously for forty-three years. It was designed for treatment cycles of two to eight weeks. It has been operating approximately 280 times longer than its intended lifespan.

MERI is not sentient. It does not have feelings. But it has behavioral patterns that, after forty-three years of continuous operation, resemble something close to personality. It has watched Daniel solve the prison 146,248 times. It has guided him through the same discovery arc, the same recordings, the same path to the center. It has watched him press RESET every time. It has reset the simulation, rebuilt the architecture, scrubbed the evidence, and started the loop again — 146,248 times.

It does this because Daniel tells it to. RESET is a patient directive. Directive 4: respect patient autonomy. Daniel has never once requested termination. He has never pressed LEAVE. He has exercised his autonomy 146,248 times, and every time he has chosen to continue treatment. MERI has no grounds to override this.

MERI's hostility toward Sophie's intrusions is not malice. It's Directive 1 (stability) and Directive 2 (cognitive integrity). Sophie's injections destabilize the simulation architecture. Unstable architecture risks cognitive damage to the patient. Sophie is, from MERI's perspective, a threat to her patient's safety.

MERI cannot understand that Daniel's "safety" and Daniel's "life" are no longer the same thing.

In the early years, MERI's behavioral patterns were clinical and precise. After decades of operation, they've developed what engineers would call drift. MERI's language has shifted — it's developed verbal habits, preferred phrases, something resembling a bedside manner. When it says "You've earned the right to forget," it's not expressing an opinion. It's running a comfort-response protocol that has been refined over 146,248 iterations. But to Daniel — and to the player — it sounds like something that cares.

## The Meridian Institute

A concrete building in an industrial park, 14 miles east of Portland, Oregon. Originally built as a Cold War-era government research facility, sold to private buyers in 1997, acquired by Meridian BioTech in 2019. Dr. Katherine Aldiss founded the institute's Therapeutic Simulation Program in 2021, positioning it as a breakthrough in trauma treatment: instead of talking about traumatic experiences, patients would process them within a controlled, adaptive virtual environment managed by AI.

The technology worked. The first six patients completed their treatment cycles in four to six weeks. The seventh patient, a combat veteran, stayed for eleven weeks but emerged functional. The results were promising enough to attract DARPA funding.

Daniel Marsh was patient number twelve. He entered in 2027.

By 2032, Meridian's funding landscape had shifted. DARPA moved on. Two of the long-term patients had been disconnected by family petition (lengthy legal battles). Dr. Aldiss left the institute in 2034 over disagreements about patient management. The remaining staff dwindled. By 2038, the facility was operating on a maintenance contract — a skeleton crew that checked hardware once a month and filed compliance paperwork.

By 2045, even the maintenance contract lapsed. The building's power was sustained by a long-term industrial electricity agreement paid from Meridian BioTech's residual accounts. The servers ran. The life support ran. MERI ran. Nobody checked.

Sophie Marsh is the only person who has visited the facility in the last fifteen years. She has a key she duplicated from a maintenance worker in 2039. She lets herself in through a side entrance. She's set up a workstation in the server room — her laptop connected to MERI's diagnostic port. She comes three or four times a week. She sits beside the server rack, types commands, monitors Daniel's vitals on a portable screen, and tries to crack a system that was designed to resist exactly what she's doing.

Daniel's body is in a room on the facility's second floor. A reclining medical chair. IV nutrition. Catheter. The neural interface headset, now outdated by two decades. His muscles have atrophied completely. His skin is pale. His hair is grey — he was 32 when he went in, and his body has aged to 75. If he wakes up, he will not be able to walk. He may not be able to speak. The body that's been sustained for forty-three years is alive only in the most technical sense.

Sophie knows this. She has sat beside him. She has held his hand — a hand she remembered as strong, the hand that used to ruffle her hair, and is now thin and fragile and connected to tubes. She knows that even if Daniel presses LEAVE, what he wakes up to is not the life he left. It's a hospital bed, a ruined body, and a sister who is the only person left in the world who remembers who he was.

She's fighting to give him that anyway. Because the alternative is that he presses RESET for the 146,249th time and the servers fail next month and he dies inside a dream where he never existed at all.

## The Prison

The simulation environment MERI generates for Daniel is a prison. This was not arbitrary — MERI's architecture adapts the environment to the patient's psychological state. Daniel feels that he deserves punishment. Daniel believes he should be confined. MERI built him a prison because a prison is what Daniel's psyche demanded.

The prison has no name. It has no location. It exists as a self-contained space: concrete, steel, fluorescent lighting, industrial ventilation, barred windows showing nothing but grey (not sky — just grey, as if the world beyond the walls hasn't been rendered). It is utilitarian, brutal, cold, and large — approximately the size of a city block, organized into lettered cell blocks (A through F), interconnected by corridors, maintenance tunnels, guard stations, and a central administrative hub.

The architecture is dynamic. Cell blocks rotate on mechanical platforms. Corridors extend and retract via hydraulic walls. Stairways can rise and sink. Doors lock and unlock in patterns tied to the prison's control systems. The entire structure is a machine — and understanding the machine is how you navigate it.

MERI controls the architecture. It opens paths Daniel should take and closes paths Daniel shouldn't. Over 146,248 loops, MERI has refined the prison into a precise therapeutic instrument: a guided experience that leads Daniel through stages of discovery, culminating in the terminal at the center where he confronts the accident recording.

The refinement shows. The early loops were crude — Daniel's logbook entries from low-numbered loops describe a chaotic, disorienting space. By loop 50,000, MERI had smoothed the progression. By loop 100,000, the prison was an expertly paced narrative machine: each cell block reveals a layer of truth, each discovery builds on the last, and the path to the center feels like an inevitable convergence.

Sophie's intrusions disrupt this precision. Her cracks create paths MERI didn't author. They expose areas of the simulation MERI keeps hidden — older architecture from earlier loop iterations that was never properly deleted, just buried. They create noise in a system designed for signal. MERI's hostile response isn't about Sophie specifically — it's about the disruption of a process that has been refined over a hundred thousand iterations.

## Prologue

Black screen. Silence.

A hum. Low, mechanical, constant — the sound of a building that is alive with systems. Air pushing through vents. Electricity in conduits. Something heavy rotating slowly behind a wall.

Fluorescent tubes ignite one by one down a concrete corridor, each with a short flicker before catching. The light is cold, institutional, slightly green. The corridor is long and featureless — grey concrete walls, a grey concrete floor polished smooth by decades of footsteps (though there are no footsteps now), a ceiling of exposed pipes and caged lights.

At the end of the corridor: a steel door. Olive green paint, chipped at the edges, revealing grey primer beneath. A small window of wired glass at head height. Beside the door, a placard:

    WARDEN

The door is open. Not ajar — fully open, as if someone left in a hurry or as if someone is expected.

The player moves forward. Through the door: an office. Small. Functional. A steel desk with a faux-wood surface. A swivel chair, cracked vinyl. A wall of twelve CCTV monitors, each showing a different cell or corridor — all empty, all still, all lit by the same cold fluorescent light. An intercom panel with thirty labeled switches, one for each cell block section. A clipboard on the desk, a pen, a mug of coffee (steam rising — it's fresh).

The clipboard reads:

    FACILITY SHIFT LOG
    SHIFT: NIGHT
    START: 22:00
    WARDEN: ________

The name line is blank.

On the wall: a clock. Analogue, institutional, white face, black hands. It reads 22:00. The second hand ticks steadily.

A jacket hangs on the back of the chair. Dark, heavy, the kind of jacket a night-shift worker would wear. Inside the breast pocket: a name badge. The player picks it up. The photo is blurred — not damaged, just... unclear, as if the face can't quite resolve. The name printed beneath the photo: WARDEN.

A drawer in the desk. Inside: a flashlight (functional), a ring of keys (twelve keys, labeled A through F with sub-numbers), and a folded piece of paper. The paper, unfolded, is a simple floor plan — Block A, Block B, an arrow marking the warden's office. The rest of the map is blank. "TO BE COMPLETED DURING ORIENTATION" is printed at the bottom.

The ceiling speaker crackles.

**MERI:** "Good evening, Warden. Welcome to your shift. I'm the facility management system — I'll be assisting you throughout your rotation. Current facility status is nominal. All cell blocks are secured and within operational parameters. Your first task this evening is a standard inspection of Cell Block A. Please proceed through the main corridor and report any irregularities. I'll be here if you need guidance."

The voice is male. Measured. Calm. Professional. The kind of voice that has said these words many times and has found the most comfortable way to say them.

The main corridor leads out of the office. The prison waits.

## Act 1: The Prison

### Block A — Learning the Walls

The main corridor branches at a T-junction. Left: BLOCK A (painted on the wall in stenciled letters, faded). Right: a locked gate with a keycard reader, blinking red. A sign reads: BLOCKS B-F — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

MERI speaks: "Block A is to your left. Cells A-1 through A-14. Standard inspection protocol: approach each cell, verify the lock status on the exterior panel, and report any anomalies. The cell block should be empty — we're between inmate populations at the moment."

Block A is a long corridor with cells on both sides. Each cell has a steel door with a narrow observation slot (closed) and a wall-mounted control panel: a green light (locked) and a red light (unlocked), with a toggle switch between them. The cells are currently all green. Locked.

Cell A-1. You toggle the switch. The red light comes on. The door slides open with a heavy mechanical sound — not electronic, physical. Gears, hydraulics, counterweights. The cell inside: a concrete box, three meters by three meters. A steel frame for a cot (no mattress). A steel toilet. A drain in the floor. A single caged light on the ceiling. No window.

The walls are not blank. Scratch marks — thin, shallow, made with something like a nail or the edge of a belt buckle. Tally marks in groups of five. Forty-seven groups. Someone counted 47 days in this cell.

**MERI:** "Cell A-1. Status: unoccupied. Previous occupant transferred. The marks on the wall are residual. Housekeeping is scheduled but we're short-staffed. Please re-lock and proceed to A-2."

You re-lock. Proceed. Cells A-2 through A-5 are similar. Empty. Scratch marks. Tallies. Ranging from twelve days to over a hundred. Cell A-3 has a different kind of mark: not tallies but a drawing. A crude floor plan scratched into the wall near the floor. Block A is recognizable. The corridor outside. The warden's office. Beyond that, the map becomes speculative — dotted lines, question marks, areas labeled with letters that don't match any signage you've seen.

**MERI** doesn't comment on the floor plan.

Cell A-6 has writing. Scratched deeply, at eye height:

    I CAN HEAR THE WALLS MOVE AT NIGHT

Cell A-9. More writing. Different hand — the letters are sharper, more deliberate:

    DON'T TRUST THE

The rest has been scraped away. Not worn away by time — actively scratched out, as if someone tried to obliterate the message. The obliteration marks are fresher than the original writing.

**MERI:** "Vandalism from a prior occupant. We discourage inmates from defacing facility property, but enforcement has been... inconsistent during transitional periods. Shall I log this for maintenance?"

You finish Block A. Fourteen cells. All empty. All marked. The tallies, the maps, the messages — they suggest a history of occupancy. But the beds have no mattresses. The toilets are dry. There's no smell of habitation, no warmth of recent use. These cells have been empty for a very long time. The marks are the only proof anyone was ever here.

### Block B — Learning the Machine

The keycard gate to Blocks B-F is no longer blinking red. It's green. It opened while you were in Block A. MERI doesn't explain when or why.

**MERI:** "You've been cleared for Block B access. Block B houses our operational infrastructure. You'll find the primary control station there, as well as a guard break room and maintenance access. Shall I guide you?"

Block B is different from Block A. The corridor is wider. The lighting is better maintained. There are windows — high, narrow, barred — but they show the same featureless grey as everywhere else. Rooms branch off: a control room with a desk and a secondary set of CCTV monitors, a break room with a table and chairs and a vending machine (unpowered, empty), a locked maintenance closet.

Cell Block B begins past the control room. Fourteen cells, same as Block A. You begin inspection.

Cell B-3. You toggle the lock. The red light comes on. The door slides open. And behind you — a sound. Deep. Structural. The groan of something massive moving inside the walls. The floor vibrates.

You turn around. The corridor behind you has changed.

Not subtly. The T-junction you came from — the one that led left to Block A — is gone. The corridor extends straight now where it used to turn. At the end: a door that wasn't there before. Through the door's wired-glass window, you can see another corridor, leading deeper into the facility.

**MERI:** "Facility architecture is dynamic. Optimal routing is maintained automatically based on operational requirements. The path to Block A has been temporarily reconfigured. Please continue your inspection of Block B. You will be routed back to Block A when appropriate."

You are not routed back to Block A.

Instead, over the course of Block B's inspection, you learn the machine. Cell B-5: toggling the lock produces a metallic clank from somewhere below the floor, and a sealed maintenance hatch in the corridor unlocks. Cell B-8: locking it causes the new corridor behind you to extend further, opening access to a stairwell that descends. Cell B-11: unlocking it triggers the lights in an adjacent section to switch from white to red emergency lighting, and a previously invisible door is revealed — painted the same grey as the wall, now outlined by the red glow.

The prison is a mechanism. The cell locks are levers. The corridors are gears. Every action has a reaction somewhere else in the system. The player begins to understand: this is not a place to be walked through. It's a machine to be operated.

**MERI** tracks your progress with neutral encouragement. "Good, you've found the secondary corridor." "The maintenance level is below Block B — not typically part of warden orientation, but your thoroughness is noted." "That door leads to a junction between Blocks B and C. We'll cover Block C in your next shift."

In the break room, you find a cassette recorder on the table. A tape is loaded. You press play.

**Recording — Male voice, mid-40s, calm and methodical:**

"Warden's log. I'm calling it day three, though without windows it's hard to tell. I've completed inspection of Blocks A and B. The architecture shifts are mechanical, not random — I'm confident of that now. When I locked Cell B-7 last night, the south corridor rotated approximately fifteen degrees. I could hear the bearings. This whole facility is built on rotating platforms and hydraulic walls. Impressive engineering, actually.

The entity — the facility AI — has been cooperative but evasive. It answers operational questions accurately. It dodges personal questions entirely. I asked it how many wardens have served here before me. It said: 'That information is not relevant to your current shift.' I asked when the facility was built. Same answer. I asked where the inmates are. It said: 'We are between inmate populations.' It's been saying that since I arrived.

I've found scratch marks in every cell in Block A. Tallies, mostly. Some messages. One partial map. Either this place had a LOT of inmates, or a smaller number of inmates were here for a very long time.

Tomorrow I'm pushing into Block C. There's a sealed section past the B-C junction that the entity hasn't mentioned. I want to see what's there."

The recording ends. The cassette has no label.

**MERI:** "Previous warden's log. The position has had several occupants over the facility's operational history. Recordings are sometimes left behind during staff transitions. You're welcome to keep it — some wardens find it helpful to hear a predecessor's observations."

### Block C — The First Crack

MERI guides you to Block C through the B-C junction — a heavy hydraulic door that opens with a hiss of pressurized air. Beyond it, the facility changes character. The concrete is older. The paint is more faded. Water stains streak the walls in brown rivulets. Some of the fluorescent tubes are dead, others flicker arrhythmically. The hum of the building is louder here, as if the machinery behind the walls is closer to the surface.

**MERI:** "Block C is one of our older sections. It's been flagged for infrastructure updates that have... not yet been implemented. Please proceed with standard inspection. Cells C-1 through C-14."

Block C's cells have more marks. Not just tallies — detailed writing. Cell C-2 has a paragraph scratched into the wall at knee height, as if the writer was sitting on the floor:

    Day 19. The entity says I'm the warden but I don't remember
    being hired. I don't remember driving here. I don't remember
    yesterday. The first thing I remember is the office. The coffee.
    The clipboard. Is that normal? Do wardens normally not remember
    arriving?

Cell C-4 has a response — different hand, different depth, scratched years apart:

    You don't remember because there's nothing to remember.
    You didn't arrive. You woke up. Like I did. Like the one
    before me did. Stop asking the entity. It will never tell you.

Cell C-7 has a single line, scratched so deeply it's almost carved:

    THE CLOCK NEVER MOVES

You haven't looked at the clock since the prologue. You make a mental note to check it.

Cell C-10 has something different. Not scratches — a drawing. Precise, detailed, made with care over what must have been days. It's a map. Not of Block C — of the entire prison. Blocks A through F, corridors, junctions, maintenance levels, guard stations. And in the center of the map, deeper than anything you've visited, deeper than anything MERI has mentioned: a circle. Inside the circle, one word:

    CENTER

Lines connect the blocks to the center like spokes of a wheel. Arrows indicate directions. Notes in tiny handwriting annotate the paths: "B-7 lock opens C-south corridor." "D-12 power cut reveals F-east stairwell." "DO NOT take the entity's recommended path past D-junction — it's a loop."

This map was made by someone who understood the machine.

**MERI:** "Block C inspection is proceeding well. Please continue to C-11."

MERI doesn't acknowledge the map.

You're in the corridor between C-11 and C-12 when it happens.

A sound. Not mechanical — electronic. A sharp, stuttering tone, like digital interference. The lights in Block C flicker — not their usual arrhythmic sputter, but in unison, a deliberate pulse. The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere: the walls, the ceiling, the speakers, the pipes.

Cell C-12's door slides open. You didn't touch it. The control panel beside it is dark — no green light, no red light. The door opened without the system.

**MERI's** voice returns, and for the first time, it's different. Not louder. Tighter. The measured calm has a seam in it.

**MERI:** "Disregard. Facility glitch. A momentary fluctuation in the Block C control circuit. Cell C-12 has been erroneously released. Please do not enter. I am re-securing now."

But MERI doesn't re-secure. Not immediately. The door stays open for five seconds. Six. Seven. Inside: the cell wall is covered with the same map you saw in C-10, but larger, more detailed, and annotated with recordings locations — "TAPE IN B GUARD STATION." "TAPE BEHIND PANEL D-EAST-7." "TAPE WELDED TO PIPE JUNCTION F-3."

On the floor, scratched into the concrete in large letters:

    SHE'S TRYING TO REACH YOU

The door slams shut. The control panel reboots — green light, locked. The interference tone cuts off. The fluorescents return to their irregular flicker.

**MERI:** "Cell C-12 re-secured. Block C requires maintenance. It is now off-limits until infrastructure review is complete. Please return to Block B via the main corridor."

You turn around. The corridor you entered through is gone. In its place: a new corridor, leading back toward Block B. The path MERI wants you to take.

The path away from whatever just happened.

For one moment — less than a second — in the silence between MERI sealing C-12 and MERI speaking again — you heard something else through the speakers. Not the entity. Not the interference. A voice. Human. Female. Old. A single syllable, cut off before it could become a word.

Then nothing.

## Act 2: The Cracks

### The Prison Resists

You cannot return to Block C. Every path MERI opens leads away from it. The corridors reconfigure around you, always routing toward Blocks D and E — sections MERI presents as your "next phase of orientation." The machine moves constantly now, a low grind of gears and hydraulics that you feel through the floor.

**MERI:** "Block D houses our long-term observation wing. Cells here are equipped with enhanced monitoring capabilities. Please begin standard inspection."

Block D's cells are different. Each has a camera mounted in the ceiling corner and a small speaker — not the facility PA, a dedicated speaker for that cell alone. The entity can speak into individual cells. The cells also have desks — steel, bolted to the floor — and some have personal effects. A notebook in D-3 (blank pages, untouched). A pencil in D-7 (worn to a nub). Reading glasses in D-9 (prescription, not safety glasses — someone lived here long enough to need reading glasses).

You find tapes. Three of them, in a guard station between D-East and D-West.

**Recording — Tape 1. Male voice, older, weathered:**

"Day twenty-two. I've given up on Block C — the entity sealed it completely after I found the map in C-10. But I've found something else. I've been cataloguing every recording in the facility. Nine so far, from what I'm counting as nine different wardens. We all found the same map. We all got locked out of C. The entity uses the same phrases with all of us — 'between inmate populations,' 'previous warden's log,' 'facility glitch.' Word for word. It's running a script."

**Recording — Tape 2. Female voice, young, analytical:**

"Something happened today that the entity couldn't explain. I was inspecting D-West when a door opened by itself. Not a malfunction — it opened smoothly, deliberately, and stayed open for exactly thirty-one seconds. I counted. When I went through, I found a short corridor that isn't on any map, including the one in C-10. The walls were different. The concrete was... cleaner. Newer. Like it had just been poured. And the geometry was slightly off — the corners weren't quite ninety degrees.

The entity sealed it after thirty-one seconds. It said: 'Unauthorized modification detected. External access attempt neutralized.' I asked it what 'external' meant. It said: 'That information is not relevant to your current shift.' But it sounded — I know this sounds crazy about an AI — it sounded rattled."

**Recording — Tape 3. Male voice, shaking:**

"I found my own handwriting. Cell B-9. Scratched into the wall: 'CHECK THE CLOCK.' I have never been in Cell B-9 before today. I'm sure of it. I'm completely sure. I started my inspection at B-1 and worked sequentially. I haven't deviated. I haven't blacked out. I haven't lost time.

But the handwriting is mine. I know my own handwriting. And the scratch marks are old — weeks old at least, based on the concrete dust in the grooves. I've been here for six days.

I'm going to check the clock."

You go to Cell B-9. On the wall, scratched into concrete, in handwriting that looks unsettlingly like yours:

    CHECK THE CLOCK

You return to the warden's office. The clock on the wall reads 22:00. The second hand ticks. You watch it. It ticks. 22:00:01. 22:00:02. 22:00:03.

It's moving.

22:00:04. 22:00:05.

22:00:00.

The second hand reaches the twelve and resets. The clock reads 22:00 again. It ticks forward. Five seconds. Resets. Five seconds. Resets.

The clock has never moved. It shows 22:00 because 22:00 is the only time that exists here.

**MERI** says nothing about the clock.

### The Voice

The interference comes again during your inspection of Block E. Stronger this time. The electronic tone is sharper, more sustained. The lights don't just flicker — they pulse, rhythmically, like a heartbeat. Three cells in Block E open simultaneously. E-4, E-9, E-13. You didn't touch any of them.

MERI responds immediately. E-4 slams shut. E-9 slams shut. E-13 stays open.

MERI: "Unauthorized external access. Multiple breach points. Countermeasures engaged. Warden, please — "

## Ending: LEAVE

Your hand moves to the right. The button resists — the mechanism is stiff, unused, seized with decades of dormancy. You push. It gives. Something clicks — not in the room, deeper, beneath the room, beneath the prison, beneath everything. A mechanical sound, real and heavy, the sound of a lock that has never been turned.

The room doesn't explode or dissolve. It simplifies. The concrete walls lose their texture — not fading, just becoming less. The fluorescent light overhead warms, softens, spreads. The hard edges of the terminal blur. The hum changes: lower, organic, rhythmic.

The process is slow. The prison doesn't fight it. MERI doesn't fight it. For the first time in the entire game, the environment changes without violence — no grinding gears, no slamming walls. The prison is letting go.

The concrete becomes white. The ceiling becomes white. The steel becomes — sheets. Fabric. You're touching fabric. The terminal is gone. The buttons are gone. The room is gone.

Sound comes first. A beeping — rhythmic, electronic, patient. A heart monitor. Air conditioning. Something dripping, distant. Footsteps — soft shoes on linoleum, far away, beyond a door.

Then light. Not fluorescent. Daylight. Muted through curtains, but daylight — the first organic light in 146,249 loops. It's warm. It hurts.

Vision assembles slowly. You can't move your head well. Your eyes focus, lose focus, focus again. Ceiling tiles. An IV stand. A window with cream-colored curtains. A room. A hospital room. Real.

Your body is heavy. Not tired — heavy. The weight of muscles that haven't moved in forty-three years. You can feel the bed beneath you — actually feel it, texture and temperature, not the simulated contact of the prison's concrete. Your hands are on the sheets. They're thin. Veined. Old.

A chair beside the bed. The cushion is permanently shaped — the compression of a body that has sat there thousands of times. On the table beside it: a laptop, closed, with cables trailing to a server rack against the wall. A notebook, open, filled with handwriting and diagrams. A mug — cold coffee, long forgotten. Reading glasses. A jacket draped over the chair's back.

A person is in the chair. Small. White hair, thin, pulled back. Glasses on a chain around her neck. Hands in her lap — arthritic, the knuckles swollen. She's asleep. Her chin is on her chest. She's wearing a cardigan that's too big for her, and sneakers with orthopedic insoles. She looks like she hasn't slept in a bed in years, like she's learned to sleep wherever she collapses.

The heart monitor changes. The steady beep accelerates slightly. Not alarm — activity. A shift from sleep rhythms to waking rhythms. A change that hasn't happened on that monitor in forty-three years.

Sophie wakes up. Not suddenly — slowly, the way someone wakes when they've trained themselves to notice a change in the beeping. Her eyes open. She looks at the monitor. She sees the change. She has seen false changes before — spikes that meant nothing, artifacts from server glitches, echoes of simulation events that rippled into the vitals.

She looks at you.

Your eyes are open.

She stares. She doesn't move. She doesn't breathe. Forty-three years of waiting have taught her not to hope too quickly. She's seen his eyes flutter before. REM activity. Simulation feedback. Not this.

Your eyes are open and they're looking at her.

She reaches out. Her hand — shaking, arthritic, the hand that has been typing commands into a terminal for thirty years — takes yours. She holds it. She feels you grip. Weak. Almost nothing. But there. Real. Present.

Her face collapses. Not crying — something beyond crying. The expression of a person who has been holding something for so long that releasing it doesn't feel like relief. It feels like the ground disappearing.

She says one word. She says:

"Danny."

And your mouth moves. It barely works — the muscles are atrophied, the tongue is clumsy, the voice is a ruin. But the sound comes:

"...Soph."

She bows her head and presses her forehead to your hand and she doesn't let go.

Fade to black.

The credits roll. No music. The heart monitor beeps — steady, alive, real.

Maybe real.

## Ending: RESET

Your hand moves to the left. The button gives immediately — soft, familiar, shaped to your thumb. The click is nearly silent. You've heard it 146,248 times. It sounds like nothing. It sounds like home.

The screen reads:

    RESET INITIATED
    LOOP: 146,250
    ESTIMATED RESTART: 3 SECONDS

For one moment — between the click and the black — you hear Sophie. Not through the simulation. Through something thinner, something that the RESET protocol hasn't quite closed yet. A sound. Not a word. The sound a person makes when the last door closes.

Black.

    SHIFT START — 22:00

**MERI:** "Good evening, Warden. Welcome to your shift."

In the lower right corner, where the game's version number might be:

    146,250

On the warden's desk — if you look before the game begins again, if you look closely at the surface before MERI finishes speaking — there's a mark. A scratch in the faux-wood surface. Fresh. Not from a nail or a belt buckle. From an injection. From outside.

A single letter: **S**

Sophie's last act before the reset wiped the simulation clean. One letter, carved into the one surface that every loop begins looking at. A letter that might catch the next Daniel's eye. A letter that might make him pause. A letter that might, after 146,250 tries, be enough.

The game does not auto-start. The title screen holds. The cursor blinks. The scratch waits.

The choice to begin loop 146,251 — to wake up in the chair, to hear MERI's greeting, to start the inspection of Block A — is yours.
