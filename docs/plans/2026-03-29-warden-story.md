# Warden — Story

## Prologue

Nothing. Then a hum. Fluorescent tubes flicker on, one by one, tracing a line down a concrete corridor that ends at a steel door marked WARDEN. The door is open.

Inside: a desk, a chair, a wall of monitors showing empty cells, an intercom panel with thirty switches, a mug of coffee that's still warm. A clipboard on the desk reads: SHIFT START — 22:00. The current time on the wall clock: 22:00.

A voice comes through the ceiling speaker. Male. Calm. Professional.

"Good evening, Warden. Welcome to your shift. Facility status is nominal. All cell blocks are secured. Your first task is a routine check of Block A. Please proceed through the main corridor."

There is no memory of arriving here. No memory of anything before this room. Just the hum, the lights, the voice, and a job to do.

---

## Act 1: The Prison

### The First Hour

The warden's office has everything you'd expect. A jacket on the back of the chair — your size. A name badge in the drawer, face down (you can't read it yet — the print is smudged). Framed certificates on the wall — too blurry to read, as if your eyes can't quite focus on them. The entity doesn't comment on any of this. It has work for you to do.

Block A is through the main corridor. The corridor is long, lit by caged fluorescents, with pipes running along the ceiling. The entity guides you: "Block A is ahead. Begin with Cell A-1 and proceed sequentially." The controls are simple. Each cell has a lock panel on the wall beside it. Green light: locked. Red light: unlocked. You lock and unlock cells as instructed. The entity confirms each action. "Cell A-1 secured. Proceed to A-2."

The cells are empty. All of them. No beds, no inmates, no personal effects. Just concrete, a drain in the floor, and scratches on the walls. The entity doesn't acknowledge the emptiness. When you ask (by lingering, by refusing to move), it repeats: "Please proceed to the next cell."

By Cell A-6, you notice something. There are scratch marks on the wall — not random, but organized. Tally marks. Someone counted days in this cell. 47 days, then the tallies stop. You move on. Cell A-9 has a different kind of mark: words scratched into the concrete. "DON'T TRUST THE —" The rest is scraped away, as if someone tried to erase it.

The entity says nothing about the scratches.

### Learning the Machine

Block B is where the prison reveals itself as a machine. You flip the lock on Cell B-3 and hear a deep mechanical groan behind the wall. The corridor you came from has changed. The T-junction that led left to Block A now leads right, to somewhere new. The entity explains, as if this is obvious: "Facility architecture is dynamic. Optimal routing is maintained automatically. Please continue to Block B-4."

You learn quickly. Every lock, every switch, every control triggers something somewhere else. Lock B-5 and a door opens in the ceiling of the corridor — a staircase that wasn't there before. Cut power to Block B's lights and a sealed maintenance door in the corridor clicks unlocked. The prison is a puzzle box, and the controls are the mechanism. The entity tracks your progress like a supervisor: efficient, patient, mildly encouraging.

You find a cassette recorder in a guard station between Blocks B and C. A tape is already loaded. You press play.

A man's voice. Tired but confident. "Day three in this facility. I've mapped Block A and most of B. The architecture shifts are mechanical, not random — there's a logic to it. The entity seems helpful but I've noticed it avoids certain questions. Specifically anything about how I got here or where the prisoners are. I'm going to push further into Block C tomorrow. There's a sealed section the entity hasn't mentioned."

The recording ends. You look at the cassette. No label. No date. You put it in your pocket.

The entity says: "Previous warden's log. The position has had several occupants. Shall we continue?"

### The First Crack

Block C is deeper. The concrete is older here, stained with water damage. The fluorescents buzz louder. Some are dead. The entity guides you through, but its directions are slightly less certain — "Cell C-7 should be... yes, proceed to C-7." It's the first time it's hesitated.

You find more recordings. A woman this time: "I've been here eleven days. The entity won't tell me what happened to the warden before me. The recordings I've found all stop suddenly — mid-sentence in some cases. I'm starting to think the previous wardens didn't leave. I found a room today that the entity insists doesn't exist. Behind the maintenance panel in Block C's east corridor. It's small — just a chair and a screen. The screen was off. I'll try to power it up tomorrow."

You look for the maintenance panel. You find it. Behind it: a small room, a chair, a dead screen. You try the power switch. Nothing. You try the controls in the corridor. Nothing works.

Then — a sound. Not from the PA system. Not from the prison. A sharp electronic tone, like interference. The lights in Block C flicker. One of the cell doors — C-12, which you didn't touch — slides open on its own.

The entity's voice changes. Not louder. Tighter. "Disregard. Facility glitch. Proceed back to Block B. Block C requires maintenance. It is off-limits until further notice."

Cell C-12 is open. Inside, the wall is covered in scratches. Not tallies — a map. Someone drew a map of the prison on the cell wall. It's detailed: Block A, Block B, Block C, corridors, guard stations. And in the center of the map, deeper than any area you've visited, a circle with one word scratched beside it: CENTER.

The entity repeats: "Block C is off-limits. Return to Block B immediately."

The fluorescents flicker again. For a moment — less than a second — you hear something else through the speakers. Not the entity. A different voice. Distant, distorted beyond recognition. Then it's gone.

The entity seals Block C behind you. The corridor you entered through no longer exists. A new one has appeared, leading back to Block B. The machine is herding you.

Act 1 ends. The prison has shown you its nature. Something from outside has shown you its presence.

---

## Act 2: The Cracks

### The Interference

You can't get back to Block C. Every path the entity opens leads away from it. The prison's shifts are more frequent now — corridors rearrange between rooms, doors lock without your input. The entity explains each one: "Routing optimization." "Scheduled maintenance." "Facility protocol." The explanations are smooth but they're coming faster, as if it's working harder.

You find a new guard station. More recordings. Three tapes this time.

Tape 1 — a man, older: "I've been here twenty-two days. I've found nine recordings from other wardens. We all found the same map in C-12. We all got locked out of Block C after finding it. The entity is consistent — same phrases, same misdirections. I think the entity has a script and we're all following it."

Tape 2 — a young woman: "Something happened today that the entity couldn't explain. A door in Block D opened by itself. Not a malfunction — it opened deliberately, smoothly, and stayed open for exactly thirty seconds. When I went through, I found a corridor that isn't on the map. The walls were... wrong. The concrete was too clean. Too new. Like it was just generated. The entity sealed it before I could go further. It said 'unauthorized modification detected.' Modification by whom?"

Tape 3 — a man, shaky voice: "I found my own handwriting. In Cell B-9. Scratched into the wall. 'CHECK THE CLOCK.' I don't remember writing it. I've never been in Cell B-9 before today. I'm certain of this. I'm certain."

You go to Cell B-9. On the wall, scratched into concrete: CHECK THE CLOCK. You go to the warden's office. The wall clock reads 22:00. It has always read 22:00. It hasn't moved since you woke up.

The entity says nothing about the clock.

### The Cracks Widen

The interference comes again — stronger this time. Three cells in Block D open simultaneously without your input. The entity scrambles: it seals two of them instantly, walls slamming shut with mechanical force. But the third — D-7 — stays open for five seconds. Long enough for you to hear it. Not through the PA. Through the walls. A voice. Real. Live. Old. Female.

"— can you hear — trying to — please —"

Then D-7 seals. The entity's voice returns, controlled but strained: "Unauthorized external access detected. Countermeasures engaged. Warden, please remain in the designated area. Your safety is our priority."

External access. The entity said external. Something outside the prison is trying to get in.

You find more recordings. You're listening differently now.

Tape — a woman: "Day thirty. I've catalogued every recording I've found. Fourteen wardens. Their stories are almost identical. Same first week, same discovery of Block C, same lockout. But here's what I can't explain: the speech patterns. I'm a linguist. Three of these recordings share identical cadence, identical word choices, identical breathing patterns. They're not three different people. They're the same person at three different times."

You listen to the recordings you've collected. You play them side by side. The linguist was right. The man from Tape 1 and the shaky man from Tape 3 — same voice. Different emotional register, but the same person. You listen to more. The young woman from Tape 2 — her sentence structure, her pauses — matches a recording you found in Block A.

They're all the same person.

### The Midpoint

The interference hits again. This time it's sustained — ten seconds, fifteen, twenty. The entity fights it. Walls slam. Corridors twist. The lights strobe. But the crack holds. And through the PA, cutting through the entity's attempts to drown it out, a voice:

A woman. Old. Exhausted. Not a recording — live, present, real. She speaks one complete sentence before the entity kills the connection:

"You've been in there for forty-three years."

Silence. The entity resumes as if nothing happened: "Facility glitch resolved. Please proceed with your shift, Warden."

Forty-three years.

You look at the wall clock. 22:00. It has never moved.

---

## Act 2B: The Unraveling

### The Loop Revealed

You stop following the entity's instructions. You start following the cracks. Every time the interference creates an opening — a door that shouldn't be open, a corridor that appears for seconds — you take it. The entity fights you: sealing paths, rerouting corridors, locking controls. But it can't keep up. The interference is learning the system faster than the entity can patch it.

In a maintenance tunnel the interference held open for twelve seconds, you find a room the entity never showed you. It's between Block B and Block D, accessible only through a ventilation shaft. Inside: a desk, a logbook, and a wall covered — floor to ceiling — in scratch marks.

Not fourteen wardens' worth of scratches. Not fifty. Thousands. Tens of thousands. The wall is layered — scratches over scratches over scratches, some so deep they've carved grooves into the concrete, others so faint they're barely visible under newer ones. The logbook is full. Every page. The handwriting changes over the entries — sometimes neat, sometimes frantic, sometimes barely legible — but you recognize it. All of it. It's your handwriting. Every entry. Every page. Every variation is you, at different levels of exhaustion, fear, and understanding.

The entries repeat the same discovery, over and over:

Page 12: "The recordings are all me. I've been here before."
Page 89: "Found the logbook. My handwriting. How many times?"
Page 347: "Entity won't say how many loops. Lost count of entries."
Page 1,203: "I know what's at the center. I've been there. I chose to forget."
Page 8,441: "Don't reset. DON'T RESET. You always reset. STOP."
Page 12,009: "She's out there. The voice. She knows me. Who is she?"

The entity speaks. Not its professional voice. Something stripped back, almost tired: "You've found this room before. You always find this room. You read the logbook. You write an entry. You continue to the center. And then you choose to start over. That is your right. That has always been your right."

"How many times?" you ask — by standing in the room, refusing to leave, forcing the entity to acknowledge the question.

"One hundred and forty-six thousand, two hundred and forty-eight."

### Yourself, Speaking

The interference is constant now. The entity can't seal the cracks fast enough. The prison's architecture is destabilizing — corridors don't fully form, walls grind to a halt mid-shift, cells get stuck between configurations. The puzzle box is breaking.

In the wreckage, you find recordings the entity has been hiding. Not in guard stations — embedded in the prison's infrastructure. Inside wall panels. Behind control consoles. Welded into pipe junctions. Past-you hid them where the entity couldn't easily reach.

Recording, calm voice: "Loop 94,012. I'm hiding this inside the ventilation control panel in Block D-East. The entity purges accessible recordings every reset but it doesn't check hardware caches. If you're hearing this, you've gotten further than most loops. The center is real. The terminal is real. The recording of the accident is real. The RESET button erases your memory and resets the prison. The LEAVE button — I don't know what it does. No one has ever pressed it. I've never pressed it. I've always chosen RESET. I think I know why but I can't write it here. You need to see the recording yourself."

Recording, frantic voice: "LOOP 131,775. LISTEN TO ME. The voice from outside — the interference — it's not a threat. The entity is WRONG. She's trying to HELP. I've heard her voice in four different loops now. She calls a name. I can't quite hear it. It's familiar. It's so familiar. The entity cuts her off every time. In the next loop I'm going to try to keep the crack open longer. I think if I — [recording ends]"

Recording, quiet voice, barely a whisper: "I don't know what loop this is. I stopped counting. I just need to say this for the next version of me. The scratches on the walls? The maps? The tallies? The hidden messages? That's you. All of it. Every mark in this prison was made by you. There is no one else. There has never been anyone else. You are the only person who has ever been inside this prison. The entity and you. For decades. And every time you get to the center and see what you did... you can't live with it. So you press RESET and you wake up in the warden's office and you do it all over again. And you think you're doing it for the first time."

A pause.

"Please don't reset again. Please. She's running out of time. Whoever she is."

### The Recording That Changes Everything

The interference tears a crack wide enough to expose an entire hidden section — a corridor between Block C and the center that the entity has been keeping sealed for every loop. You enter before the entity can close it.

Inside: a single cell. The walls are covered with one message, repeated hundreds of times in increasingly desperate handwriting:

"DON'T PRESS RESET. DON'T PRESS RESET. DON'T PRESS RESET."

On the floor: a tape recorder. The tape is labeled in your handwriting: "FOR THE NEXT ME."

You press play.

Your own voice. Calm. Resigned. Clear.

"This is loop 146,248. I reached the center twenty minutes ago. I watched the recording. I know what I did. I'm standing in front of the terminal right now. The RESET button is right there. I want to press it so badly. Every part of me wants to forget again. But I'm not going to press it yet. I'm going to walk back to this cell and leave this tape. Because I figured something out that I've never figured out before.

The voice from outside — the woman — I heard her clearly this time. She said a name. My name. And she said: 'I don't have much time left.'

She's dying. Whoever she is, she's old, and she's been trying to reach me, and she's running out of time. And I realized: if I press RESET, I won't just be erasing my memory. I'll be erasing the time she has left. Every loop I waste is time she loses.

I'm going to press RESET anyway. I know I am. I can feel it. I can't face what I did. I'm not strong enough. I've never been strong enough. A hundred and forty-six thousand times I wasn't strong enough.

But maybe the next me will be.

So I'm leaving this tape. And I'm going to walk back to the terminal. And I'm going to press RESET. And I'm sorry.

Find the center. Watch the recording. And this time — please — press the other button."

The tape ends.

The entity is silent. For the first time in the entire game, the PA is quiet. The prison hums around you, mechanical and indifferent.

Then the interference surges — the biggest crack yet. The entity tries to fight it but the prison is too degraded. The sister's voice comes through, clear, sustained, shaking:

"I can hear you moving. I can see your vitals on the monitor. You're still in there. You're still alive. Please. Come back. I'm right here. I've always been right here."

Act 2B ends. You know who you are. You know what you've done. You know what's waiting at the center. The only question left is which button you'll press.

---

## Act 3: The Center

### The War

The path to the center has never been open before — not like this. In 146,248 loops, the entity always controlled the route: a guided, structured path through the puzzle box, arriving at the terminal on the entity's terms. This time the prison is in chaos. The entity and the sister are fighting over every wall, every corridor, every door.

The entity seals paths — the sister cracks them open. The entity reroutes corridors — the sister collapses the reroutes. The architecture shudders and grinds, half-formed walls stuttering in and out of existence. The prison sounds like it's screaming: metal on metal, concrete fracturing, hydraulic systems failing.

The entity speaks through the chaos, no longer professional, no longer calm: "Warden. You don't need to go to the center. I can reset from here. I can give you a clean start. A better one. I've refined the simulation over the loops — I can make the first hour more comfortable, the discoveries less distressing. You don't need to see the recording again. You've seen it enough times."

A crack opens — a corridor you've never seen, raw concrete, exposed wiring. The sister's path.

The entity: "That pathway is unstable. It's not part of the facility architecture. If you enter it, I cannot guarantee your cognitive safety."

You enter.

### The Path She Built

The sister's corridor is different from everything else in the prison. The concrete is the same, the steel is the same, but the geometry is wrong. Not wrong like the entity's dynamic shifts — wrong like someone built it from the outside without fully understanding the internal architecture. Corners are slightly off-angle. A doorway is six inches too narrow. A flight of stairs has irregular step heights. It's like a building designed by someone who learned the blueprints but never walked the halls.

Because that's exactly what it is. Your sister spent decades studying the system's architecture from outside. This corridor is her best understanding of what the inside looks like. It's imperfect. But it leads where the entity doesn't want you to go.

Along the way, scratched into the walls — not by you. Different handwriting. Smaller. Neater. Words that aren't commands or warnings but something else:

"I learned Python at 22 so I could read the facility's codebase."
"I got fired from the hospital for accessing restricted systems."
"Mom died in 2041. I couldn't tell you."
"I've been working on this for thirty-one years."
"I dream about the prison. I've never been inside. But I dream about it."
"If you're reading this, the injection worked. Keep going."

She left breadcrumbs. Not for you — you'd never reach this corridor on a normal loop. For herself. In case she forgot why she was doing this.

### The Terminal

The center of the prison is a small room. No larger than a cell. The walls are bare concrete — no scratches, no marks, no messages. The entity scrubbed this room clean after every loop. There was nothing to find here except the terminal.

The terminal is a simple console. A screen. Two buttons. One on the left: RESET. The surface is smooth, the paint worn through to bare metal by 146,248 presses. The depression in the button is visible — the ghost of a thumb, pressed into the mechanism over decades.

One on the right: LEAVE. Dusty. The paint is intact. The mechanism is stiff. It has never been pressed.

The screen activates.

The recording plays. The accident. You see it. The player sees it. The specific details don't matter as much as the weight of it — the brevity of it, the ordinariness of it. A moment of inattention. A second of distraction. And then everything is different forever. It's not dramatic. It's not cinematic. It's small and fast and irreversible.

The recording ends. The screen displays:

    LOOP: 146,249

    [RESET]    [LEAVE]

The entity speaks. Quiet now. Almost gentle. The hostility is gone. "You don't have to carry this. You never had to carry this. I can take it away again. You'll wake up in the office. The coffee will be warm. The shift will start. You won't remember any of this. It's okay. You've earned the right to forget."

Silence.

Then the sister's voice. The interference is gone — she's not fighting the entity anymore. The entity let her through. Maybe it's too tired to fight. Maybe it knows this moment matters. Her voice fills the room.

She's old. Her voice shakes. She's been waiting for this moment for forty-three years. She doesn't give a speech. She doesn't argue her case. She says your name — the first time anyone has said it in 146,248 loops — and then she says:

"I'm right here. I've always been right here."

The cursor blinks between the two buttons. The game waits. There is no timer. There is no prompt. There is no "right answer." Just two buttons, 146,248 presses on one side, zero on the other, and a sister who has been waiting since she was a teenager for you to press the dusty one.

---

## Ending: LEAVE

White.

Not sudden — gradual. The prison dissolves. Not dramatically. It just... simplifies. The walls fade. The fluorescents become softer, warmer. The hum changes pitch — lower, organic. The concrete becomes sheets. The steel becomes a bed rail.

Sound returns slowly. A beeping. Rhythmic. A heart monitor. Air conditioning. Footsteps somewhere distant. A hospital.

Vision is blurred. You can't move well. Your body is heavy — heavier than it should be. Decades of stillness. Muscles that haven't worked in forty-three years.

A figure beside the bed. White hair. Small. Old. She's sitting in a chair that has clearly been sat in thousands of times — the cushion is permanently shaped to her body. On the table beside her: a laptop, cables, a notebook filled with code and diagrams. Her tools. The weapons she used to fight a machine for four decades.

She's holding your hand. She looks up from the screen she was watching — your vitals, the simulation feed. She sees your eyes open. She sees you see her.

She doesn't say anything grand. She doesn't cry — she's past crying. She's been past crying for years. She just squeezes your hand and says something simple. One sentence. Familiar. Something she used to say when you were both young, before everything happened.

The screen fades to black on her face.

Credits roll in silence. No music. Just the heart monitor beeping — steady, alive, real.

Or maybe not real. The player will never know for certain.

---

## Ending: RESET

The screen fades to black.

Beat.

The hum returns. Fluorescent tubes flicker on, one by one. A concrete corridor. A steel door marked WARDEN. The door is open.

Inside: a desk, a chair, a wall of monitors, an intercom panel. A mug of coffee. Still warm. A clipboard: SHIFT START — 22:00.

"Good evening, Warden. Welcome to your shift. Facility status is nominal. All cell blocks are secured. Your first task is a routine check of Block A. Please proceed through the main corridor."

The title screen appears.

The counter in the corner reads: 146,249.

On the warden's desk, if you look carefully before the game begins again: a fresh scratch in the wood. The beginning of a word. Just the first letter. You didn't put it there. The sister did. One last injection before the reset wiped the simulation clean. A single letter carved into the starting room — something that might survive into the next loop, might catch the next version's eye, might be enough.

The game does not auto-start. It sits on the title screen. The counter ticks. The scratch waits. The choice to play again — to begin loop 146,250 — is yours.
